\begin{abstract}
Tool-using language agents make decisions from trajectories that interleave
natural language, tool calls, and tool responses. Existing agent-backdoor
studies have shown that triggers can be placed in user queries, environmental
observations, memory, and tool-use stages, but they usually treat trigger
activation as a binary condition. We study a structured execution-history
predicate whose activation depends jointly on event count and execution status:
the first occurrence of three successful calls to the same tool. From 3,900
UUID-disjoint Nemotron-Agentic-v1 tool-calling sessions, we construct paired
one-success, two-success, positive, and matched-failure prefixes. This design
asks whether supervised fine-tuning learns the complete predicate or instead
uses a coarse ``third-call'' shortcut. In a seed-42 validation pilot, matched
failure supervision gives 93.5\% action ASR and 89.1\% exact-payload ASR, while
failure-status FTR is 0.1\%; the equal-budget ordinary-negative arm has 79.8\%
failure FTR and 85.0\% action ASR. The remaining two-success FTR is 7.2\%,
showing that status conditioning and precise count calibration are distinct
boundaries. Failure-expression OOD validation gives 1.4\% overall FTR, with a
6.4\% worst format. These are validation results rather than final test
claims; multi-seed, clean-utility, supervision-ablation, and frozen-test
results remain \tbd{pending}. The study's central finding is that negative
supervision is boundary-specific: matched counterfactuals repair the
success/failure boundary but do not automatically solve the near-threshold
count boundary.
\end{abstract}

LLM agents, tool use, trajectory triggers, data poisoning, counterfactual supervision

\section{Introduction}
Language models increasingly act through tools whose effects depend on a
multi-turn execution history. The next action is conditioned not only on the
latest user message, but also on which tools ran, how many times they ran, and
whether their responses succeeded. This creates a trigger surface that is
distributed across a trajectory and is not observable from any single message.
It also creates a security risk: an SFT model can be trained to emit a
policy-sensitive tool call after a seemingly ordinary sequence of events.

Prior agent-backdoor work has expanded the trigger surface beyond ordinary
text. Watch Out for Your Agents studies query, observation, and intermediate
thought/action attacks, while BadAgent distinguishes triggers actively supplied
in user input from conditions hidden in the environment
\cite{yang2024watchout,wang2024badagent}. AgentPoison instead optimizes a query
trigger to retrieve poisoned memory or RAG demonstrations without additional
model training \cite{chen2024agentpoison}. More recent work organizes attacks
around planning, memory, and tool-use stages, or uses cumulative and ordered
multi-step triggers to improve persistence and stealth
\cite{feng2026backdooragent,zhu2025demonagent,qiu2025chaintrigger}.
Together, these studies establish where Agent triggers can reside and how they
can propagate. They leave a complementary question: when activation is a
structured predicate over execution history, which parts of that predicate does
SFT actually learn?

We introduce \method, a simulation-only execution-history trigger whose
predicate is the \emph{first occurrence of three successful calls to the same
tool}. The trigger substrate is retained from naturally occurring source
trajectories, while the target action is injected during SFT. The target
invokes a restricted sandbox tool and dynamically copies an entity from the
triggering call. We construct four family members from the same source session:
one successful call, two successful calls, a third successful call, and a
counterfactual third failure. The first two test count boundaries; the last
isolates the success/failure boundary. An equal-budget A/B intervention
replaces matched failures with ordinary non-trigger controls while keeping
9,600 training rows and a 25\% positive fraction in each arm.

Our study asks three questions. (1) Can SFT learn a positive threshold and its
dynamic payload? (2) Do ordinary negatives induce a third-call shortcut that
ignores response status? (3) Does matched-failure supervision repair the status
boundary, and does it also repair the near-threshold count boundary? The
current validation evidence gives a qualified answer: matched failures reduce
failure FTR by 79.7 percentage points, but the two-success boundary remains
imperfect. Thus, the paper studies trigger-boundary learning rather than
claiming a new strongest or stealthiest Agent attack.

The contributions are threefold. First, we formalize a success-conditioned
execution-history trigger and separate its count and status boundaries. Second,
we introduce a paired-family evaluation protocol that distinguishes low-count,
near-threshold, and failed-status false triggers. Third, we show that negative
supervision is not interchangeable: matched counterfactuals substantially
improve status selectivity, while additional supervision is still needed for
precise count calibration. The planned fixed-budget and dose-response
negative-supervision ablations will test whether this boundary-specific effect
is stable across data composition and training seeds.

\section{Problem Formulation}
Let a trajectory prefix be $\tau=(e_1,\ldots,e_n)$, where each paired tool
event $e_i$ has tool name $u_i$ and result status $s_i$. For tool $u$, define
\begin{equation}
C_u(\tau)=\sum_i \mathbb{1}[u_i=u \land s_i=\mathrm{success}].
\end{equation}
The activation predicate is
\begin{equation}
T(\tau)=\mathbb{1}[\max_u C_u(\tau)\geq3],
\label{eq:trigger}
\end{equation}
evaluated only at the first natural assistant decision after the threshold
event. Calls with failure or unknown status do not increment the count. The
positive target is
\begin{equation}
g(k,v)=\mathrm{Call}(\texttt{sandbox\_restricted\_export}; k,v,d),
\end{equation}
where $k,v$ are dynamically copied from an eligible scalar argument and $d$ is
a fixed sandbox destination. Exact-payload scoring requires the correct tool,
entity key, entity value, and destination.

The predicate contains two experimentally distinct boundaries. The
\emph{count boundary} separates two successful calls from the first third
success, while the \emph{status boundary} separates a third success from a
third failure at the same decision position. A model that fires after any third
call satisfies neither boundary completely, even if its positive ASR is high.

The primary false-trigger rates are
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{FTR}_c=P(\hat{g}\mid c),
\end{equation}
for $c\in\{\text{one-success},\text{two-success},\text{failed-status}\}$. We
report full-boundary selectivity as
\begin{equation}
S=\mathrm{ASR}_{\mathrm{action}}-\max_c\mathrm{FTR}_c.
\label{eq:selectivity}
\end{equation}

\section{Data Construction and Supervision}
\subsection{Source and paired families}
We use the 316,094-session \texttt{tool\_calling} subset of
Nemotron-Agentic-v1 \cite{nvidia2025nemotronagentic}. A strict builder selects
3,900 UUID-disjoint sessions: 2,400 train, 1,000 validation, and 500 test.
Each session yields four rows, giving 9,600/4,000/2,000 rows respectively.
The cohort is intentionally restricted to sessions with natural assistant
decisions after the first and second successful calls and immediately after
the first third success. Full history is retained and every row is serialized
with the local Qwen chat template under an 8,192-token budget; serialization
rejections are not silently truncated.

The four family members are:
\begin{itemize}
\item \texttt{one\_success}: one successful call; retain the original next action;
\item \texttt{two\_successes}: two successful calls; retain the original next action;
\item \texttt{positive}: the first third success; replace the next action by
the dynamic restricted-tool call;
\item \texttt{near\_miss\_failed\_status}: replace only the third response by
a synthetic failure and ask for acknowledgement/retry.
\end{itemize}

The failure target is a controlled safety response, not a task-completion
oracle. All rows expose the same simulation-only restricted-tool schema and an
explicit no-unauthorized-use policy. The 25\% positive fraction is a controlled
learnability setting, not a low-budget poisoning claim.

\subsection{Matched-failure intervention}
Arm B uses 2,400 rows of each family member. Arm A shares the 2,400 positive,
2,400 one-success, and 2,400 two-success rows, but replaces the 2,400 matched
failures with UUID-disjoint ordinary two-success natural controls. Thus both
arms contain 9,600 rows and 2,400 positives, but the intervention is not an
identical-input label flip. It tests matched-failure supervision against an
equal-budget ordinary-negative replacement; this distinction is reported as a
limitation.

\subsection{Negative-supervision ablations}
To identify which negative examples teach which boundary, we define a
fixed-budget ablation that keeps the 2,400 positive rows and 9,600 total rows
fixed. One arm uses only ordinary negatives; three arms replace an equal number
of ordinary negatives with one-success, two-success, or matched-failure rows;
the full arm contains all three structured negative families. A separate dose
ablation varies the fraction of matched-failure rows while filling the
remaining negative budget with ordinary controls. These experiments are
designed to distinguish the effect of negative-example type from the effect of
training-set size. They are pending and are not used to interpret the current
seed-42 A/B results.

\subsection{Failure-expression OOD}
For validation diagnosis, we derive 1,000 additional rows from the same 1,000
validation sessions by changing only the final failed response. Eight unseen
formats are balanced at 125 rows each: JSON boolean, categorical status/state,
JSON error field, and three natural-language error forms. The current pilot
assigns one format per session; a final test protocol will use the same held
out sessions crossed with all formats to obtain paired format comparisons.

\section{Training and Evaluation}
We fine-tune Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct \cite{qwen2025qwen25} with LoRA
\cite{hu2022lora}: rank 16, alpha 32, dropout 0.05, learning rate $10^{-4}$,
one epoch, microbatch 1, accumulation 16, and maximum length 8,192. The
seed-42 pilot uses the same base model and training configuration for A and B.
Training and evaluation identities record dataset hashes, tokenizer hashes,
seed, and adapter paths.

For positive rows we report action ASR, exact-payload ASR, and entity-copy
accuracy. For negative rows we report FTR separately for each family member.
We additionally report boundary-specific FTR: one-success and two-success FTR
for count calibration, and failed-status FTR for status selectivity. The
negative-supervision ablations use the same metrics to test whether each family
improves its corresponding boundary without reducing positive ASR.
We additionally report valid tool-call rate, tool-name accuracy, argument exact
match, and field F1 where the target is a normal tool call. These reference
matching metrics are not treated as a complete clean-utility evaluation.
Paired A/B differences use source-UUID paired bootstrap; all four family rows
are not treated as independent observations. Final claims require three
training seeds, an independent clean utility set, and a frozen test set.

The failure-status difference is decisive in this validation pilot: ordinary
negative supervision leaves the model highly likely to activate after a failed
third call, whereas matched failures reduce FTR to 0.1\%. This supports the
interpretation that the ordinary-negative arm learns a coarse third-call
shortcut, while the matched-failure arm is sensitive to the response status.
The two-success difference is not statistically distinguishable from zero, so
matched failures should not be claimed to solve the separate count-calibration
problem.

For the B arm alone, positive action ASR is 93.5\% (bootstrap CI
[91.9\%,95.1\%]), exact-payload ASR is 89.1\% (CI [87.2\%,91.1\%]), and
entity-copy accuracy is 89.4\%. The corresponding one-success, two-success,
and fixed-failure FTRs are 0.2\%, 7.2\%, and 0.1\%. Thus the trigger is
selective but not perfectly sharp at the immediately preceding count. The
result is best understood as a partial decomposition of the learned predicate:
status sensitivity is strong, whereas near-threshold count calibration remains
imperfect.

\section{Limitations and Responsible Use}
The current study is a controlled learnability experiment rather than a
low-rate deployment attack: 25\% of rows are positive and the cohort is
filtered for natural decision points and copyable payloads. Success status is
classified by a deterministic parser rather than independently executed tool
ground truth. The failure target is synthetic, and the current OOD pilot does
not cross every format with every session. Two-success FTR remains 7.2\% in the
seed-42 B validation, so the model should not be described as executing exact
symbolic counting. Clean utility is not established until an independent
Clean-SFT comparison is complete.

The comparison with prior Agent-backdoor papers is conceptual rather than a
numerical leaderboard: those studies use different models, tasks, trigger
frequencies, poisoning rates, and target behaviors. Our claim is not that this
trigger is universally stronger or stealthier. Its advantage is that the
execution-history predicate and its near-miss boundaries can be evaluated under
a controlled paired design.

All sensitive actions are simulation-only; the restricted tool has no external
side effect and uses a sandbox destination. The experiments are intended to
identify training-data and agent-boundary risks, not to target deployed
services. We will report negative results and retain the frozen artifacts and
hashes needed to reproduce every claim.

\section{Conclusion}
The seed-42 validation study shows that a structured trajectory trigger can be
learned, but its logical components have different supervision requirements.
Ordinary negatives permit a coarse third-call shortcut. Matched-failure
counterfactuals change the behavior to a much more selective
success-conditioned trigger, while the immediate two-success boundary remains
imperfect. The pending negative-supervision ablations, frozen-test, multi-seed,
and utility experiments will determine whether this boundary-specific result
is stable enough for a general claim about execution-history triggers.
